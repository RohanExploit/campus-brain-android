## RAG-MicroSim: A Hybrid Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Market Micro-Simulation Framework for High-Frequency Trading Analysis
### Abstract
High-frequency trading (HFT) methods work on sub-second time scales and often cause complicated market dynamics that are difficult to explain. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has evolved as a strategy to ground large language models (LLMs) with factual context, including domain-specific financial data[1][2]. However, RAG systems often rely on historical records or textual data and can struggle with rare or hypothetical scenarios[3]. We present RAG-MicroSim, a novel hybrid architecture that augments RAG with an integrated micro-simulation engine. RAG-MicroSim dynamically generates synthetic limit order book (LOB) states and trade events on demand, which are used alongside retrieved historical data. real-time risk assessment and anomaly identification, and permits testing of possible algorithmic behaviors not apparent in past logs.We go into detail on RAG-MicroSim's architecture, market microstructure simulation techniques, and integration plan with the RAG pipeline. In conclusion, we present an assessment strategy that contrasts RAG-MicroSim with common RAG baselines and illustrate use-case situations such forensic investigation of flash crashes, regulatory audits, and rogue algorithm discovery.
### Introduction
With the ability to execute hundreds of orders per second, high-frequency trading algorithms have emerged as a major force in contemporary markets. Risks like flash crashes—sudden, sharp price movements that seem unrelated to fundamentals—can be increased by their quick, automated operations. For instance, a single sell algorithm played a major role in the 2010 Flash Crash, which saw a 6% price decline in a matter of minutes [4]. Tens of thousands of flash crash and "mini flash crash" occurrences were found between 2006 and 2011[5], which consistently demonstrate how intricate algorithmic interconnections can cause market instability. Stability depends on comprehending these occurrences and diagnosing HFT techniques, which is challenging because black-box models are opaque.Although LLMs have been used in financial analysis, there are issues with trust and regulations because of their opaque decision-making process [6]. In fact, the necessity of model interpretability in finance has been highlighted in recent work, particularly for trading and compliance duties [6]. Hybrid AI systems that support model reasoning with specific examples and evidence are motivated by these worries.

## Mathematical and Statistical Validation of the RAG-MicroSim Framework for High-Frequency Trading Anomaly Detection
### Introduction to Market Microstructure Complexity and Artificial Intelligence Interventions
The structural evolution of global financial markets has been defined by the transition from human-mediated open outcry systems to fully electronic, continuous double auction matching engines. Within this modern paradigm, High-Frequency Trading (HFT) operates at the absolute frontier of latency, executing complex algorithmic strategies on sub-second, and increasingly sub-millisecond, timescales. HFT algorithms do not operate in isolation; they function as a highly interconnected, reactive ecosystem where the submission, execution, or cancellation of a single limit order can trigger a cascade of automated responses. While empirical studies acknowledge that high-frequency market making generally improves price efficiency and narrows bid-ask spreads under normal macroeconomic conditions, the same algorithmic interconnectivity is capable of generating profound market fragility. Extreme microstructural phenomena, including quote crumbling, latency arbitrage, spoofing, and systemic liquidity evaporation—such as the events witnessed during the 2010 Flash Crash—demonstrate how rapidly the non-linear dynamics of HFT can decouple asset prices from their fundamental valuations.
The forensic auditing and real-time monitoring of these high-frequency environments present formidable epistemological and computational challenges. Traditional econometric models struggle to capture the non-stationary, high-dimensional nature of limit order book (LOB) tick data. In response, the financial industry and regulatory bodies have increasingly turned to Artificial Intelligence (AI) and deep learning architectures, such as Transformer networks and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), to identify microstructural anomalies. However, while pure deep learning models achieve high statistical accuracy in anomaly classification, they suffer from a debilitating "black box" paradox. Their multi-layered, non-linear decision-making processes remain entirely opaque, rendering them unsuitable for regulatory compliance, forensic explanation, or actionable risk management, all of which demand strict causal interpretability.
To bridge the gap between computational power and human interpretability, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been introduced into financial analytics. Yet, standalone LLMs are inherently limited by their training corpora, lacking the dynamic context required for real-time market analysis, and they are notoriously prone to generating plausible but mathematically unfounded hallucinations. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) emerged as a mitigating architecture, designed to ground LLM outputs by retrieving factual, historical context from vectorized databases prior to generation. While RAG systems excel at summarizing historical logs, they exhibit a fundamental vulnerability when applied to HFT anomaly detection: they are strictly bounded by empirical history. RAG systems rely entirely on semantic similarity matching; therefore, they cannot evaluate rare, unprecedented, or counterfactual scenarios—such as a novel, previously unseen rogue trading algorithm—because the exact sequence of events does not exist within the static retrieval corpus.
